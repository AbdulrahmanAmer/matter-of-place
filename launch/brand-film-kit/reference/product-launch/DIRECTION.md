# How to use this film (the operator, 15 October 2026)

This film, its source, its direction below and everything from the session that made it are study material, not a
template. In the operator's words: "it is not to be used as a locked-in-place template. It is using the same process
and being on the same level of creativity and design with a few things to be locked in place ... it works as materials
to study on how to make something."

So: learn the process (code-built 3D, a moving camera, light, a code-made score, typeset titles, the narrator in
launch/voice) and the level of craft, then design each video, reel, post and carousel fresh at that level. Only a few
signature moves are fixed so everything reads as Matter of Place, such as the zoom into the logo as a door. The full
renders (MP4 with and without the voice, the cleaned voice) are on Google Drive in
My Drive\Matter of Place\Brand\Product Launch film; the 13 stills (one per shot) and the contact sheet are in
delivered/stills here. Its titles are burned into the 16:9 picture, so a vertical reel is rendered from the source at
1080 x 1920 rather than cropped from the MP4.

## 1. What was asked for

The client's own words are quoted exactly as written.

### The first brief, 2 October

> okay make a dynamic 15 second motion graphics video that shows what an incredible motion designer you are for a business that is called Matter Of Place.

> the video I want you to go all out I will give you the logo with this prompt too you can break it apart to use it do whatever you see now go

The logo came with this message. Between those two lines the client described the company at length. These are the parts that shaped the film, in the client's words:

> Matter of Place is a selective real estate media company. It finds exceptional homes, tells their story properly, publishes them, and distributes them to an audience that cares about architecture, interiors and place.

> It is not a brokerage, a listings portal, a lead-generation shop, an ad agency or a software product. It does not sell homes. It sells attention, presentation, editorial context and distribution. In its own words: we curate it, we frame it, we publish it, we distribute it.

> It works in three markets only: California, New York and Florida. It covers existing residential property only. No new developments, no global listings, no memberships. Price never equals merit: a home is chosen for what it is, not for what it costs.

> A real estate agent or a property owner submits a home through the website, with its details, its story and its photographs. An editorial team reviews it. Review comes before any payment, and not every home is accepted. An accepted home is edited, published as a feature on matterofplace.com, shared on Instagram, X and LinkedIn, and included in Place Notes, the newsletter that goes out every two weeks.

> Four exposure packages, paid only after acceptance: The Feature at 295 dollars, The Reach at 695 dollars (the recommended one), The Campaign at 1,495 dollars, and Five Features at 1,250 dollars. Paid media placement is offered separately, from 250 dollars, with a 15 percent management fee and a 100 dollar minimum. It makes no promise of leads, buyers or sales.

> How it sounds and looks. Calm, brief and specific, with no hype. A quiet palette of obsidian, bone, ivory and sandstone, and editorial serif type. Three tests gate everything it publishes: the owner of a 15 million dollar home is comfortable here; someone who is not buying would still follow; a top agent says "we got you in".

## 2. Every version shown, what the client said, and what changed

### Version 1: the first brand film, 15 seconds, 2D (2 October)

It was shown as a page that played the film and as `matter-of-place-brand-film.mp4` (1080p, 60 frames a second). It is not in this package, because Product Launch replaced it.

It was flat 2D motion graphics in the brand palette. The idea came from the emblem: the gap between its two planes is a house-shaped doorway. A door of light opened in the dark on "Not every home is accepted." and the view went through it. Inside, one home was curated, framed, published and distributed, then came the three markets and "Price never equals merit.", and the view pulled back out as the doorway became the emblem above the wordmark. The homes were drawn as architectural models, with no photographs.

The client said: "can we do something even better more advanced more artistic more with more details better motion graphics with camera angles something even better go all out on this one the SPACE is the limit here PROVE YOURSELF and name that video as product launch or something".

What changed: the story, the palette and the emblem-as-door idea were kept, and the treatment was replaced. A new film was built in real 3D so the camera could move through space: a dolly through the doorway, a crane up to a plan view, an orbit around the chosen house, tracking shots past the markets and the packages. It gained physical detail: polished floors with reflections, light you can see, depth of field, motion blur and soft shadows. It grew from 15 to 30 seconds to give those moves room (the second brief did not set a length), and it took the client's name for it, Product Launch. What was rejected was flatness and a modest level of ambition, not the idea.

### Version 2: Product Launch, working cut (2 October)

Shown on its own page, playing live: 30 seconds, real 3D, ten shots, with sound. It was labelled as a working cut. The client did not comment before the next version.

### Version 3: Product Launch, finished cut (2 October)

The same page, updated after a finishing pass. The pass added polished floors with reflections in the hall, in New York and on the packages stage. It turned the declined homes into plan drawings as they sink. It added nightfall, with lit windows and four lanes of cards each cut to the shape of its channel. It retimed the ending so that "Price never equals merit." and the logo both get room. These changes came from the finishing pass, not from feedback.

### Version 4: Product Launch, the MP4 (2 October)

This is `matter-of-place-product-launch.mp4` in this package: the same film rendered offline at 1080p and 60 frames a second, with depth of field, motion blur and softer shadows. Notes given with it: the feature headline "A house that keeps the afternoon.", the closing line "Submit a home · matterofplace.com" and small labels such as "Edited by an editorial team" were written for the film and are not from the brief; the prices on screen come from the brief and will date; the parent company does not appear.

The client said nothing about the picture and asked for the voice-over next. On 4 October they said "what we did is good and I like it". The picture has not changed since this version.

### Version 5: the voice-over script, first draft (2 October)

53 words, about 21 seconds of speech, one line for each shot: "Not every home is accepted." "Agents and owners bring homes to us." "Our editors review each one. Payment waits." "What we accept, we frame." "We publish it as a feature." "And we send it out." "Three markets." "California." "New York." "Florida." "Four packages, paid after acceptance." "Price never equals merit." "Matter of Place."

The reading notes asked for a low, level, unhurried voice, "like an editor reading a masthead rather than an advert", at about four syllables a second, with the first line and "Price never equals merit" timed to the same words on screen.

The client said: "the script is too bland and it just says what's on the screen".

Why it failed: it narrated the picture. Every line repeated a title or described the shot, so the voice told the viewer nothing they could not already read.

### Version 6: the voice-over script, rewritten (2 October)

This is the script that was voiced. It has 58 words and about 20 seconds of speech. It argues the brand's position and leaves the facts to the screen. It opens on price and closes on the name.

| Line starts | On screen | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| 0.6 s | The door opens: "Not every home is accepted." | Every home has a price. |
| 2.5 s | Through the doorway | It's rarely the interesting part. |
| 5.3 s | Rising over the submissions | A modest house can make the cut. |
| 7.7 s | The declined homes sink | A grand one might not. |

| 28.0 s | The logo | It's a matter of place. |

The reading notes: dry and level, with the wit left in the words; underplay every line, with no wink. "Editors" lands as the frame squares up at about 11.5 s, and "never" lands at about 25.4 s as the word drops into the line on screen. Florida plays with no voice, and there is a beat before each second sentence. The last line is the name, read plainly as the wordmark settles. Two alternative lines were offered and not used: "A listing tells you what a home costs. It rarely tells you what it is." and "Three markets. We'd rather know a place than cover it."

The client did not object. They asked for the script as a spreadsheet and had the voice generated from it.

### Version 7: the script as a spreadsheet (2 October)

`matter-of-place-voice-over-script.xlsx`: the same lines, cues and notes. Nothing changed.

### Version 8: the film with the voice, first delivery (3 October)

The client explained that the voice was generated and could not be recorded again, then sent the WAV. It was cleaned and mixed without moving any timing, because the file already sat on the cues. Six small clicks inside words were removed. The boomy low end was reduced by about 6 dB: the lowest part of the voice carried about three quarters of its energy, which booms on headphones and disappears on phone speakers. The "s" sounds were turned down by 4 to 7 dB, and every line was brought to the same loudness. The music dips about 9 dB under each line, a little more under the last line and where a hit lands inside a line, and returns to full level for the accents on the cuts between lines and for Florida. It was delivered as the film with the voice and as the cleaned voice on its own.

The client said, on 4 October: "right now you can see that what we did is good and I like it it can be improved overtime".

### Version 9: corrected film with voice and corrected cleaned voice (4 October)

While the method was being tested, a seventh click was found that the first delivery had missed, at 12.8 s in the word "listing". It was repaired and nothing else changed. These two corrected files replaced the earlier two, and they are the ones in this package.

### After that (15 October)

The working method, the exact render process and the film as code were packaged and sent. The film did not change.

### What this history says

The client welcomed ambition, and it went into craft: real camera moves, real light and fine detail, never louder graphics. The palette, the serif type and the calm tone survived every version.

The words on screen carry the facts and the voice carries the point of view. The one piece of work the client turned down was the script that let the two say the same thing.

The picture was accepted without a change, so the final film is the reference for style. Follow its rules, not the first film's.

## 3. The visual rules of the final film

### The idea

The emblem is a doorway. The film walks through what the company does as a selective editor. A door opens that not every home gets through. The submitted homes appear as architectural models in a gallery. One is kept and the rest sink, leaving their plan drawings. The kept home is framed, published as a page and sent out at night. Then come the three markets, the four packages and the principle, and the camera goes back through the door, which closes into the logo. Everything on screen is a model, a page or light. Nothing is photographed.

### Format and shot list

1920 × 1080, 60 frames a second, 30 seconds. Ten shots on an 80 BPM grid: one beat is 0.75 s, and every shot starts on a beat. The markets shot is three views joined by hard cuts.

The rules behind it:

- Backgrounds are never pure black or pure white. Pure white appears only on the Florida house and in the brief camera flash in the frame shot.
- Display lines are only ever obsidian or bone. Ink follows the paper: obsidian on light, bone on dark. Sandstone is kept to small accents: a tag, a rule, a square, and the rings of light under The Reach.
- The value flips at every hard cut. The film goes dark (the door), light (the gallery), dark (night), sand (California), dark (New York), light (Florida), dark (the packages), light (the wall) and dark (the lockup). A series of reels or posts should keep that alternation.
- At nightfall the gallery's paper turns from bone to near-black over 1.7 s (15.15 to 16.85 s). The big words never fade through the colour of the background on the way. The dark set rolls up and out while a bone set rolls up behind it, around 16.0 s. The small labels change from obsidian to bone in 0.26 s at the same moment.

### Type

Two families, both free on Google Fonts:

- **Newsreader**, weight 300 (Light), roman and italic, for every display line. The film uses its 72 pt optical size, the display cut. Weight 400 appears only in the headline printed on the page mock.
- **Jost**, weight 500 (Medium), for every label, counter and price, always in capitals and letter-spaced. Weight 400 appears only in the lockup tagline. Jost is never used in italic.

The rules:

- Each title has one italic, and it is the word that carries the meaning: "accepted.", "submits a home.", the verbs "curate", "frame", "publish" and "distribute", and "never". The rest of the line is roman.
- Display lines are in sentence case and end with a full stop when they are sentences ("Price never equals merit.", "Four exposure packages."). Names have no full stop ("California").
- Labels are capitals, letter-spaced between 0.2 and 0.36 em, most often 0.28 em.
- There are no other typefaces, no bold, no underlines, and no outlines, shadows or glows on type.
- The wordmark is always the supplied logo artwork, never typed.

### Camera

- One continuous camera runs from 3.75 s to 17.25 s, through submissions, curate, frame, publish and distribute. There are no cuts in it; each move overlaps the next.
- Hard cuts happen only at 17.25, 18.75, 20.25, 21.75 and 24.75 s: into and between the markets, into the packages and into the wall. The two passes through the door, at 3.75 s and 27.0 s, are hidden under a full-frame wash of light.
- Each scene has its own camera, and no move is carried across a hard cut.

The moves, in order (the angles are vertical fields of view, with the matching full-frame lens):

- **The door.** A slow, low approach that drifts onto the centre line and looks up at the door (0 to 3.3 s), then an accelerating push through the doorway into the light (2.95 to 3.77 s). 34°, about a 33 mm lens.
- **Submissions.** A low glide over the plinths (3.4 to 5.7 s), widening from 42° to 38° (about 26 to 29 mm), with shallow depth of field.
- **Curate.** A crane up to a straight-down plan view (5.3 to 6.75 s, narrowing to 30°), then a slow drift while the editor's box scans.
- **Frame.** A descent that becomes an orbit around the chosen house (8.9 to 11.5 s), narrowing to 22° (about 52 mm) and ending square-on, so the frame lines up with the screen. The camera then stops and the lens closes down for the photograph (11.5 to 12.2 s).
- **Publish.** A slow pull back as the page forms (12.2 to 14.9 s).
- **Distribute.** A crane up and back as night falls (14.95 to 17.25 s), coming to rest exactly on the cut.
- **The markets.** One move each, spread over the whole 1.5 s and starting and ending at rest: California a low truck to the right, New York a crane up the facade, Florida a high arc. 30° to 32°.
- **Packages.** Close on the first sheet, then back and across as the others rise. 28°.
- **Principle.** Almost still: a 1 m slide and a 2.5 m push toward the wall over the whole shot, at 30°, with everything sharp.
- **Lockup.** A one-second pull straight back from inside the light to the closing door (27.0 to 28.0 s), at 20° (about 57 mm), then a two-second hold.

How it moves:

- Every move eases in and out. Nothing starts or stops with a jolt. The only linear motion is the drift over the plan.
- Depth of field is used while the camera travels, and it is closed for the photograph, the wall and the logo.
- The motion blur is real, from a 180° shutter at 60 frames a second, and the titles get it too.
- There is no shake, no handheld drift, no whip pan and no spin. The horizon stays level, apart from a brief tilt of up to about 9° while the camera turns to look straight down and back up.

### Pacing and cut rhythm

- 80 BPM, one beat every 0.75 s, and every shot starts on a beat.
- The shots last 5, 4, 4, 3, 4 and 3 beats, then 2, 2 and 2 for the markets, then 4, 3 and 4: 40 beats, 30 seconds.
- The shape is one long breath first (13.5 s of continuous camera), then a quickening (a hard cut every 1.5 s through the markets), then a slowing down: the packages, an almost still principle and a held logo.
- Every cut and every change of shot lands on an accent in the music.
- Titles are timed from the start of their shot. The first line of a new shot starts 0.02 to 0.1 s after the cut and is fully in within 0.8 s.

### How titles enter and leave

- **Entering.** A line rises into an invisible mask the size of the line. It travels up 1.14 times its own size, 200 px for a 176 px line, and arrives in 0.62 to 0.8 s with a strong ease out: it covers 97% of the distance in the first half and settles slowly. There is no fade and no scaling.
- **Groups.** Lines in a group follow each other 0.06 to 0.12 s apart: "We" at 6.50 s, "curate" at 6.58 s, "it." at 6.66 s.
- **Changing.** When one word replaces another, as the verbs and the header labels do, the old word rolls up out of its mask while the new one rolls up into it, together, in 0.62 s with an ease in and out.
- **Leaving.** A title either rolls up out of its mask or stays until a hard cut and goes with the picture. Display lines do not fade out, with one exception: the door sentence fades in 0.3 s just before the camera goes through the door. Small labels fade.
- **Small labels.** They fade in over 0.4 to 0.6 s, or roll in like the big lines. They never bounce or scale.
- **The key word last.** In the principle line, "Price", "equals" and "merit." rise first, 0.06 s apart. A gap opens in the middle over 0.55 s, and "never" rises into it 0.44 s after the cut.
- **Pinned labels.** Labels pinned to objects ride the camera exactly: the plinth numbers, IN REVIEW then ACCEPTED, EDITED, PUBLISHED then DISTRIBUTED, and the channel names on the card lanes.
- **The lockup.** The door closes and the emblem settles into its two flat brand tones. The thirteen letters of the wordmark fade up and rise 16 px, from the centre outward, 0.03 s apart, while their spacing closes from 20% wide to normal. The tagline's letter-spacing tightens from 0.52 to 0.36 em. A short sandstone rule grows from the centre, and the footer fades in last. Everything has settled by 29.1 s and holds to the end.
- **Rules.** Hairlines draw from left to right (the header) or grow from the centre (the lockup).

### Light

- **The door.** There are no lamps. A warm white light behind the door pours through as it opens, and you see the light itself: thin layers of haze, rays, a pool on the floor with a soft edge, and dust that glows only inside the beam. The floor is black and polished, so the light and the sentence are reflected in it.
- **The gallery by day.** A soft, warm, low sun (#FFF9F0), at first from behind on the left, then swinging round to the front left as the house is framed. It never quite stands still. A white sky and a sand-coloured ground fill the shadows. Every model has a soft contact shadow. The floor is the colour of the background, so there is no horizon: a seamless studio.
- **Dusk, 15.15 to 16.85 s.** The background goes from bone to near-black. The sun warms (#FFD7A3), then turns blue (#B4BFD2) and drops. The chosen house's windows glow warm and the floor turns to polished glass.
- **California.** A warm, low, raking sun (#FFE6C0) from the left, about 15° high, with long shadows.
- **New York.** Night: a weak, cool moonlight (#C4CCDA), lit windows and street lamps in warm amber, and a wet street that reflects them.
- **Florida.** A high white sun, short shadows, a bright ivory world.
- **Packages.** No lamps: a dark polished stage, a soft pool of light under each sheet, and slow rings of sandstone light under The Reach. The reflections fade out before they reach the title.
- **The wall.** An even, soft light, with a patch of afternoon sun in the shape of a window, two panes by three.
- Shadows are soft everywhere. The soft shadows, the depth of field and the motion blur are real, made by averaging many renders of each frame, not added afterwards.
- Every frame has a soft round vignette, strong in the dark scenes and almost nothing in the light ones, and a fine monochrome grain that changes 24 times a second. There is no colour grade, no lens flare and no bloom apart from the glow of the door.

### What is never done

- No photographs, footage or stock. Every home is a matte architectural model with no textures. The "photograph" in the film is a render of the model, and the page is typeset in the film.
- No people, cars, animals or real furniture.
- No parent company: its name and logo never appear, and there is no endorsement line.
- No promise of leads, buyers or sales, and no claims of results. The only numbers on screen are the package prices and the paid media line.
- No hype: no exclamation marks and no superlatives.
- No camera shake, whip pans, spins or speed ramps.
- No transitions other than hard cuts, continuous camera moves and the wash of light at the door. No dissolves, wipes, slides, glitches or zoom punches.
- No title that bounces, scales, spins or types on letter by letter, and no title with a shadow, glow or outline.
- No pure black or pure white backgrounds, and no colours outside the palette.
- No more than one italic word or phrase in a title.
- Words never pass through the colour of the picture, and reflections never run under a title.

## 4. The sound rules

### The score

- It is original and synthesized: 30 seconds at 80 BPM, in D major. It is calm and warm. It is not a track with drums; it is a sequence of soft chords over a quiet heartbeat.
- The instruments are soft pads (two slightly detuned voices through a gentle low-pass filter, slow to swell and slow to fade); a soft sine mallet, like a muted vibraphone, for the melody notes; a low felt-kick pulse on every beat; a very quiet shaker on the off-beats; deep sub hits on the big cuts; small high ticks for the editor's scanning and the page typesetting; and soft rising air into the transitions. All of it sits in a long reverb, 3.2 seconds at about a third wet, with gentle compression.
- There is one chord for each shot, changing on the cut. The door has open fifths on D, room tone and air, and two mallet notes for the two halves of the sentence. Submissions is D. Curate is B minor, with four ticks as the editor scans and two notes as one home is accepted. Frame is G. Publish is A, with a shutter click and then ticks as the page sets. Distribute is B minor, with a rising run of eight mallet notes as the cards fly out. The markets are D, B minor and G, two beats each, each with a sub hit. The packages are A, with one rising note as each sheet comes up. The principle is G.
- The pulse starts at 3.75 s and stops at 24.75 s, so the principle line plays without it. "Never" is met by a single mallet note at 25.5 s.
- The ending: rising air through the door from 26.6 s, a final D chord swelling from 27.75 s, a sub hit and four mallet notes from 27.9 s, and the tail fading out over the last 0.7 s.
- The music-only film measures -16.1 LUFS, with a true peak of -2.3 dBTP.

### The voice

- It is a generated voice, low and deep (its pitch sits around 88 Hz). The client supplied it as one WAV already laid out on the cues.
- The tone is dry and level and underplayed, with the wit left in the words. There is no lift at the ends of lines, no wink and no sell.
- The pace: 58 words in about 18.6 seconds of speech, which is about three words a second while it speaks. For about 11 seconds of the film there is no voice at all. Each sentence lasts 1 to 2 seconds.
- Silence is part of it. Florida plays with no voice. There is a beat before each second sentence: "We're not brokers.", half a second, "We're editors." The first line starts 0.6 s in, once the door has begun to open.
- The sync points: "editors" lands as the frame squares up, at about 11.5 s, and "never" lands as the word rises into the title, at about 25.4 s. The last line starts at 28.0 s, just after the final hit.
- It is counterpoint. The voice never says what is on screen. The titles carry the facts (the process, the markets, the prices) and the voice carries the position (price is not merit; editors, not brokers; a listing waits, a feature travels).
- The cleaning in the delivered WAV: seven splice clicks repaired; the boom below 140 Hz reduced, from 85% of the voice's power to 71%, with two gentle low shelves at 145 Hz; the "s" sounds tamed between 3.2 and 6.2 kHz; an upsampling artefact above 12 kHz removed; every sentence levelled to the same loudness, within 0.2 LU; peaks limited. No noise reduction was needed, because the gaps were already silent.

### How loud the voice sits over the music

- The voice is set 2 dB above the music's own loudness, and then the music dips under every line: 9 dB under most lines, 10.5 dB under "Even our map is edited." (it sits on the market cuts) and 12 dB under the last line (it carries the name).
- The dip starts 0.3 s before a line and is complete as the line begins. The music stays down for 0.15 s after the line, then comes back up over one second, or faster when an accent is coming, so that it is fully back for the accent on the next cut. Pauses shorter than 0.7 s stay down.
- Accents that fall inside a line are pressed down with the rest of the music, by design: those at 3.75, 6.75, 9.75, 12.0 and 18.75 s. All the other accents play at full level.
- For the last line the dip is timed by hand, 27.93 to 28.10 s, so the final chord lands first and the voice follows. (Next time, give that closing hit 0.3 s to itself before the voice comes in.)
- Music peaks in the speech range are pressed back when they come within 6 dB of the voice.
- The result: every line is 12 to 15 dB above the music in overall loudness, and at least 8 dB above it in the speech band, 300 Hz to 4 kHz. The film with the voice measures -16.0 LUFS with a true peak of -3.5 dBTP, the same loudness as the music-only film.

## 5. Making a 15-second vertical cut

### Render it again rather than cropping it

A 9:16 window cut from the 16:9 film is 608 × 1080 px. To fill a 1080 × 1920 frame it has to be enlarged 1.78 times, which looks soft. Worse, the titles are burned into the picture and laid out across the wide frame: the header spans 1,680 px and the principle line 1,549 px. A tall crop slices through them in nearly every shot.

The right route for this film is to render the shots again at 1080 × 1920 from the code package, with each camera aimed for the tall frame and the titles set again for it. Almost every title is in one file (`src/film/7_titles.js`); the door sentence and the page are in the door and gallery files, and each scene's camera is in its own file. The package's README gives the render commands. That is a job for a developer or for another session with Claude. If only the MP4 can be used, crop the picture where no title is burned in, and set every title again on top at full resolution in the same type.

### Keep the grid

15 seconds is 20 beats at 80 BPM. Cut only on beats, on multiples of 0.75 s. Do not speed the footage up to make it fit: the easing and the motion blur are part of the calm, and sped-up footage looks nervous. Choose whole sections instead.

### A structure that fits exactly

Three stretches of the film, joined by two edits that both fall on beats and on changes of shot, so the music's accents come with them:

| In the cut | Beats | From the film | What it shows | Voice (times in `matter-of-place-vo-cleaned.wav`) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 to 3.75 s | 5 | 0.00 to 3.75 s | The door opens on "Not every home is accepted." and the camera goes through into the light. | "Every home has a price." (0.63 to 2.44 s) |
| 3.75 to 9.75 s | 8 | 6.75 to 12.75 s | The plan view, where one home is kept and the rest become drawings ("We curate it."), then the orbit that squares up into the photograph ("We frame it."). | "We're not brokers." (9.63 to 10.75 s), "We're editors." (11.26 to 12.21 s) |
| 9.75 to 15.00 s | 7 | 24.75 to 30.00 s | "Price never equals merit.", then back through the door into the logo. | "It was never a matter of price." (25.03 to 26.99 s), "It's a matter of place." (28.00 to 29.49 s) |

Keep each voice line at the same distance from its picture as in the film, so the sync points survive. In the cut, a line goes at its time in the WAV for the first stretch, 3.0 s earlier for the middle stretch and 15.0 s earlier for the last. "Editors" then lands as the frame squares up at about 8.5 s, and "never" lands at about 10.4 s. That is 7.3 seconds of voice in 15, a little more silence than the film has. Adding "It's rarely the interesting part." (2.44 to 4.26 s in the WAV) brings it to 9.1 seconds, the film's balance; it runs across the first edit just as it runs across the cut in the film. One improvement on the film is worth making here: start the last line 0.2 s later than its slot, at 13.2 s instead of 13.0 s, so the final hit has 0.3 s to itself before the voice comes in.

What this leaves out, and why: the packages and prices, because they date and cannot be read at phone size (give them their own post); the markets, because three shots of 1.5 s do not survive being squeezed into a short story (film 17.25 to 21.75 s makes a good separate short); and the night section.

Two details at the edits:

- At 3.75 s the film is fully covered by its wash of light (#F7F4EE), and it reveals the next shot as the wash fades over half a second. In the cut the next frame comes from 6.75 s, where there is no wash. Start the middle stretch under the same wash, full at the edit and clearing over 0.5 s with an ease out, so the door still opens into light.
- The second edit joins two light scenes, which the film itself never does. Keep it exactly on the beat, so the change of chord and the stopping of the pulse carry the change.

### The sound for the cut

- Build it from the two stems, not from the finished mix. Cutting the mix would chop sentences in half, because at 3.75 s and 12.75 s a line is being spoken.
- The music is the sound of `matter-of-place-product-launch.mp4`, which is the score alone. Cut it at the same two points as the picture with a crossfade of 20 to 30 ms. Accents in the score start exactly on the beat, and there are notes at 3.75 s and 12.75 s, so make each edit a few milliseconds before the beat so that no attack is clipped.
- Lay the voice lines from `matter-of-place-vo-cleaned.wav` and duck the music as the film does: 9 dB under each line, falling over 0.3 s before it and coming back over a second after it, and 12 dB under the last line, with the final chord landing before the voice.
- Aim for the film's loudness, -16 LUFS integrated, with peaks below -1 dBTP. If a platform wants it louder, raise the whole mix, never the voice alone.

### Framing for 9:16

- The door is already a tall shape and fits a vertical frame well. Put "Not every home" above the doorway and "is accepted." below it, instead of on either side.
- In the plan view, turn the camera 90° so the grid of plinths, five across and four down in the film, becomes four across and five down, and set "We / curate / it." above it.
- The photograph is a 3:2 landscape rectangle. Keep it in the middle of the frame with the verb stack above it.
- The verb stack works as it is. Its widest word, "distribute", is 655 px wide at 176 px.
- Break the principle line into three lines: "Price / never / equals merit." At 150 px the widest of them, "equals merit.", is 823 px, which fits a 1080 px frame.
- In the lockup keep the emblem and the wordmark centred, and bring the wordmark down to about 760 px wide to keep a margin.

### Safe areas

On Reels-style players the top 14% of a 1080 × 1920 frame (about 270 px) and the bottom 35% (about 670 px) are covered by the account name, the caption and the audio label. The buttons sit over the right 21% (about 230 px) of the lower part, and each side needs about 65 px. That leaves a central area of about 950 × 980 px for anything that must be read. These overlays change, so check the platform's current template before finishing. In the film the market names and the packages line sit low in the frame (baselines 956 and 968 of 1080); in a reel, move them up into the safe area.

### Type at phone size

The display sizes carry over to the tall frame. The small captions do not: at 11 to 19 px in a frame 1080 px wide they cannot be read on a phone. Anything that must be read needs about 32 px or more, in Jost 500 capitals letter-spaced about 0.2 em. Keep the small labels only as texture, or leave them out, and keep at most one short header label in each stretch.

### Captions, cover and timing

- Many people watch with the sound off, so the titles must tell the story alone. The film is built that way.
- If the voice needs captions, prefer the platform's own captions, which viewers can turn off. If they must be burned in, set them in Jost 500 at about 36 px, in sentence case, in obsidian or bone like the titles, inside the safe area, and never in Newsreader, so they do not compete with the titles.
- The first frame of the film is almost black. Choose the cover separately: `02_door-title_02.00s.jpg` or `13_lockup_29.50s.jpg`.
- Add nothing the film does not have: no app text effects, stickers, emoji, trending audio, speed ramps or extra transitions.
- Check the facts that date before posting: the prices, the three markets and the web address.

### For still posts

- The stills are 1920 × 1080. A 4:5 post (1080 × 1350) cut from one needs an 864 × 1080 window enlarged 1.25 times, which holds up. A square post needs no enlargement. A 9:16 story cut from a still is as soft as the reel crop, so compose it instead.
- Most stills have titles burned in. Crop so a title stays whole, or leave it out entirely; never cut through words.
- Lay posts out the way the film lays out its frames: one display line in Newsreader 300 with a single italic word, small letter-spaced Jost labels, the wordmark at top left over a hairline, margins of about 6%, obsidian on light or bone on dark, and sandstone only as a small accent. In a grid, alternate dark and light posts the way the film alternates its scenes.
